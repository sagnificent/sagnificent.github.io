Introduction to Bayesian Data Analysis

Why Bayesian at all?

Statistics as a subject has not been around for a long time. It had started to materialise as a discipline when gamblers realised they could mathematically quantify "chance", which facilitated their study of gains and losses. Now how we define "chance" is what leads us to the two schools of thought in statistics.

The Frequentist Version

Toss a coin 100 times. Count the proportion of heads among the 100 trials. Here's a simple code to run this: 

set.seed(1)

heads <- sample(c(0, 1), size = 100, rep = TRUE)

proportion_heads <- cumsum(heads) / (1:100)

plot(1:100, proportion_heads,
     xlab = "No of Trials",
     ylab = "Proportion of Heads",
     main = "Cumulative Proportion of Heads (Fair Coin Toss)",
     ylim = c(0, 1),
     type = "b")

abline(h = 0.5, col = "red", lty = 2)

The plot will show something like this:

As the number of trials increases, we can see the proportion of heads occurring converges to 0.5 — the theoretical probability, or "chance", that heads will appear when the coin is tossed. Thus we now quantify the chance that heads appear as 0.5. The frequentist argues:

"As the number of trials increases, the proportion of a certain event occurring converges to its true probability."

This fact is supported by a very important theorem in probability called the Law of Large Numbers, which states that:

If \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) are IID random variables with finite mean and variance, then \(\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{X_n} - \mathbb{E}(X)| > \epsilon) \to 0\) for \(\epsilon > 0\).

Exercise: Use the LLN to show the above probability converges.

Now how does a Bayesian define this notion of chance?

The Bayesian Version

A Bayesian defines "chance" as "belief in the occurrence of that event". The probability that heads appear when a coin is tossed means "I believe that there is a 50% chance of heads occurring when the coin is tossed".

We interpret \(P(A)\) as the strength of the argument A, where:

  • \(0\) implies \(\bar{A}\)
  • \(1\) implies \(A\)
  • \((0,1)\) implies the degree of confidence in our argument.

You may ask, "Why do we care about this interpretation at all? After all, the calculations will remain the same!"

Yes, probability calculations in the Bayesian and frequentist views are almost the same, but the Bayesian interpretation of probability has more practical applications. In our day-to-day life we use probabilities to express our confidence in a statement. A 30% chance of rain hardly means that out of 100 days, around 30 of them will make us traverse through the "ocean of knowledge" through the ISI campus! It means I am not so sure about it raining, but it might — "given the weather of the past few days". Moreover, I can't afford to fail half of my papers to tell my friends, "I have a 50-50 chance to pass this course"!

In such scenarios, we want to interpret probability as belief. Later we will learn that every Bayes rule is admissible (in other words, no other rule is strictly better in every scenario). This is the reason why we are interested in the Bayesian interpretation of data.
